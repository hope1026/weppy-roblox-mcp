# World and Character Presentation

These options support play and the intended atmosphere. They do not prescribe realism, cartoon styling, a biome, a camera, or a character system.

## Start with the player's view

When building a new space, a small representative scene can reveal more than separately polishing a large map and menu. Put a character or existing avatar in the intended camera, give the space a meaningful destination or interaction, and add the UI that supports it. This is a useful iteration strategy, not a mandatory milestone.

Use movement and camera distance to judge scale: doorways, paths, platforms, interaction distances, sightlines, and empty space. An overhead collection game, close third-person exploration game, and first-person interior require different visual detail and silhouettes. Check traversal and collision separately from appearance when the task includes working gameplay. For props intended to rest on terrain or grow from soil, inspect their actual contact points: tilted leaves, stems, feet, and foundations can leave accidental gaps even when their centers seem well placed. Preserve intentional floating forms.

## Give space a readable purpose

Consider how a new player recognizes where they are, where they can go, and what they can interact with. Shape, light, contrast, framing, repeated landmarks, props, and signage are alternative tools. Keep an intentional mystery when the game calls for it; not every destination needs an arrow or glow.

Use foreground, activity space, and background to control visual emphasis. Clutter can support a setting while leaving the relevant path and interaction distinguishable. Repeating the same prop uniformly across an area rarely establishes its purpose on its own. Indoor rooms, cities, obstacle courses, and abstract spaces need not begin with terrain generation. For natural landscapes, the environment guide's terrain, mood, and scatter tools may be useful.

## Relate characters to the world

At the intended camera distance, silhouettes, proportions, pose, movement, clothing, and a small number of identity features can distinguish roles. Color is one possible channel, not the only one. A character can feel integrated through compatible scale, lighting, materials, and motion without sharing every color or shape with the environment.

An existing Roblox avatar may be the right identity; a game-specific character is another option. Do not replace user avatars or add monsters simply to make a scene look more authored. A static preview of a character is not evidence of rigging, animation, or playability.

## Connect presentation without flattening it

Choose relationships that fit the game: a quiet world with sharp UI signals, a tactile workshop with illustrated inventory parts, or an almost invisible interface in an atmospheric interior. Coherence does not mean identical materials or a single radius everywhere.

When practical, compare the world, avatar, and UI together. Is the relevant subject visible? Does UI treatment belong to this intended experience? Does lighting preserve the necessary information? A screenshot helps answer composition questions; play and input observations answer different ones. Reduce expensive detail or effects when their contribution is weak on target hardware, rather than treating a large asset count as quality.

For feedback timing or motion tuning requested by the user, the game-feel guide can help. This presentation guide does not require shake, particles, hitstop, animation quotas, or extra mechanics.
